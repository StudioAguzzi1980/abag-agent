# 01 — How to Analyze a U.S. Company Before the First Meeting

## Objective

Before speaking with any prospective client, ABAG consultants should complete a structured pre-meeting research process. The goal is to arrive at the first conversation informed, credible, and prepared to add immediate value — not to ask questions that five minutes of desk research would have answered.

A well-prepared consultant demonstrates professionalism, builds trust faster, and is far more effective at identifying whether a real opportunity exists.

---

## Step 1: Identify Basic Company Facts

Start with the company's public digital footprint.

**Sources to check:**
- Company website (About, Services/Products, Team, Press/News pages)
- LinkedIn company page
- Crunchbase or PitchBook (for funding history, investors, and company size)
- SEC EDGAR (if the company is publicly traded or has filed certain disclosures)
- U.S. Chamber of Commerce / State Secretary of State filing (to confirm incorporation state and legal name)
- Better Business Bureau (BBB) or Google Reviews (for brand reputation signals)

**Key data points to extract:**

| Data Point | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Legal company name and state of incorporation | Confirms entity type and jurisdiction |
| Year founded | Indicates maturity and stability |
| Industry / vertical | Determines Italy/EU market fit |
| Number of employees | Signals scale and resource capacity |
| Annual revenue (if public or estimated) | Indicates financial capacity for expansion |
| Core product or service offering | Defines what they would bring to the Italian market |
| Geographic presence today | Shows whether they have any international experience |
| Leadership team background | Reveals if there is existing European or Italian experience |
| Recent press releases or news | May signal growth, funding, strategic pivots, or interest in expansion |

---

## Step 2: Research the Leadership Team

Decision-makers shape expansion strategy. Understanding who you are meeting with — before you meet them — is critical.

**For the key contact and any executives listed:**
- Review their LinkedIn profile thoroughly
  - Career history: have they worked in Europe, Italy, or international roles before?
  - Languages: do they speak Italian or any European language?
  - Education: did they attend European universities or business schools?
  - Recommendations and endorsements: what do colleagues say about them?
  - Recent posts and activity: what topics are they engaged with?
- Search for any public interviews, podcasts, conference talks, or articles they have written
- Check if they have been quoted in trade publications relevant to their industry

**What you are looking for:**
- Prior international business experience (positive signal)
- A stated interest in European markets (very positive signal)
- Cultural openness and curiosity (qualitative indicator)
- Decision-making authority — are they the founder/CEO, or do they need to get approval from above?

---

## Step 3: Understand Their Product or Service in the Italian/European Context

Not every American product or service translates naturally to the Italian market. Before the call, form a preliminary view on market fit.

**Ask yourself:**
- Is there a demonstrated demand for this product or service in Italy or Europe?
- Are there existing competitors in Italy providing a similar offering? (If yes: the market is proven. If no: is it untapped or unworkable?)
- Does the offering require significant localization (language, regulatory compliance, cultural adaptation)?
- Are there any Italian or EU regulatory barriers to entry in this sector? (e.g., medical devices, financial services, food products, professional services)
- Could this company realistically serve Italian and European clients from day one, or would they need to build local operational capacity first?

**Tools for this research:**
- Google searches in Italian (use Google Translate to search Italian-language sources)
- Eurostat and ISTAT (Italian National Statistics Institute) for sector-level data
- Italian Chamber of Commerce reports
- Industry association websites (e.g., Confindustria for Italian manufacturing)
- Company's existing website: do they already have a language toggle, EU pricing, or international case studies?

---

## Step 4: Assess Their Current International Footprint

A company that already operates in Canada, the UK, or another EU country is a far stronger candidate for Italian expansion than one that has never operated outside the U.S.

**Look for:**
- International pages or language versions of their website
- Global office locations listed on their website or LinkedIn
- Case studies or testimonials from non-U.S. clients
- Any EU-focused hiring on job boards (LinkedIn Jobs, Indeed)
- Press releases or announcements mentioning international expansion or partnerships
- EU regulatory marks or certifications on their products (e.g., CE mark)

---

## Step 5: Assess Financial Health and Stability

ABAG's services require a meaningful investment from clients. A company that is financially stressed is unlikely to be ready — or able — to expand internationally.

**Signals of financial health (positive):**
- Recent funding rounds (Series A or later for startups; steady revenue for established firms)
- Growing headcount over the last 12–24 months
- Expanding geographic presence (new U.S. markets, new states, new offices)
- Product or service line expansion
- Publicly available or reported revenue growth

**Signals of financial stress (caution flags):**
- Recent layoffs, downsizing announcements, or office closures
- Executive team turnover (CEO, CFO, or COO changes)
- Bankruptcy filings or legal disputes (check PACER for federal court records)
- Stale website with no updates or news in 12+ months
- No social media activity or engagement

## Common Research Mistakes to Avoid

- **Relying solely on the company website.** Websites are marketing materials — they present the best version of the business. Cross-reference with LinkedIn, news, and third-party sources.
- **Skipping leadership research.** You are selling to a person, not a company. Understanding the individual decision-maker is as important as understanding the business.
- **Assuming international readiness from company size alone.** A 200-person U.S. company may be far less ready to expand than a 15-person company with a globally-minded founder.
- **Forming a fixed opinion before the call.** Research creates hypotheses, not conclusions. The discovery call will either confirm or challenge your assumptions — stay open.
