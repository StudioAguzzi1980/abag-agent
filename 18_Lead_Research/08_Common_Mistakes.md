# 08 — Common Mistakes Consultants Make During Qualification

## Overview

The qualification process is where business development is won or lost — not in the proposal or the closing. Mistakes made during qualification lead to wasted time, misaligned engagements, difficult client relationships, and missed revenue. This document catalogs the most common qualification mistakes made by consultants at all experience levels, with guidance on how to avoid them.

---

## Mistake 1: Talking More Than Listening

**What it looks like:** The consultant spends the majority of the first meeting presenting ABAG's services, delivering a structured pitch, and describing what ABAG does — rather than asking questions and understanding the prospect's specific situation.

**Why it happens:** Nervousness, enthusiasm, or a genuine belief that the prospect needs to hear about ABAG before they can decide if they're interested.

**Why it's costly:** You learn nothing about the prospect. They feel talked at, not understood. And the "pitch" you deliver is generic, not tailored to their actual needs — which makes it far less compelling.

**The fix:** Enforce a personal rule: speak no more than 40% of the time during a first meeting. Prepare your questions in advance. When you feel the urge to pitch, ask a question instead.

---

## Mistake 2: Failing to Ask About Budget

**What it looks like:** The consultant goes through an entire discovery call without asking the prospect about their budget or investment parameters. They then spend days preparing a detailed proposal, only to hear "we didn't realize it would cost that much."

**Why it happens:** Fear of being perceived as too transactional, assumption that the prospect knows what things cost, or a belief that the budget conversation can wait until the proposal.

**Why it's costly:** Without understanding budget, you cannot assess financial capacity (20 points in the scoring system), tailor the scope of the proposal, or determine whether the lead is actually viable. You waste time preparing proposals for non-buyers.

**The fix:** Ask the budget question directly and frame it professionally: *"To make sure I propose something that works for your situation, it's helpful to understand the investment range you're working with — what have you budgeted for the setup and first year?"*

---

## Mistake 3: Failing to Identify the Real Decision-Maker

**What it looks like:** The consultant builds a strong relationship with a contact who is enthusiastic, engaged, and knowledgeable — but who turns out to be a researcher, an analyst, or a project manager without budget authority. When the proposal is presented, it has to "go upstairs" for approval to someone ABAG has never spoken with.

**Why it happens:** The enthusiastic contact is pleasant to work with, and asking "are you the decision-maker?" can feel awkward or presumptuous.

**Why it's costly:** You may spend weeks building a relationship with someone who has no ability to say yes. When the proposal goes to the real decision-maker cold, it often stalls.

**The fix:** Ask the question early, tactfully: *"When a project like this moves forward, who are typically the key people involved in the decision? Would it make sense to include them in our next conversation?"*

---

## Mistake 4: Accepting "Yes" as a Signal of Intent

**What it looks like:** The prospect says "Yes, this sounds interesting," "Yes, we're definitely thinking about Italy," or "Yes, we should move forward" — and the consultant interprets this as a buying signal and invests heavily in preparing a proposal.

**Why it happens:** Positive language feels encouraging. It is human nature to interpret agreement as commitment.

**Why it's costly:** "Yes, this sounds interesting" is not a buying commitment. It is a polite response. Many consultants have learned this the hard way after presenting elaborate proposals to companies that were never truly committed.

**The fix:** Test seriousness by asking commitment-specific questions: *"If we put together a proposal and everything looks good, what would the timeline for moving forward look like? Is there anything that could prevent this from happening in the next 60 days?"*

---

## Mistake 5: Over-Qualifying or Dismissing Prospects Too Quickly

**What it looks like:** The consultant encounters a prospect who doesn't immediately fit the ideal client profile — they're a smaller company, they have a modest budget, or their expansion plan is still early — and dismisses them as not worth pursuing.

**Why it happens:** A desire to focus on "big wins" and an impatience with long sales cycles.

**Why it's costly:** Some of ABAG's best long-term client relationships started as small initial engagements. A company with €20,000 today may have €200,000 to invest in two years. Burning a bridge with a "not yet ready" prospect can close a door permanently.

**The fix:** Every "not yet ready" prospect deserves a professional, respectful exit that keeps the relationship alive. Offer a useful resource, maintain occasional contact, and keep them in the nurture pipeline.

---

## Mistake 6: Assuming Enthusiasm Means Readiness

**What it looks like:** The prospect is passionate about Italy — they may even have Italian heritage, have visited multiple times, or be genuinely in love with the idea of doing business there. The consultant interprets this enthusiasm as readiness and fast-tracks them into the sales process.

**Why it happens:** Enthusiasm is contagious. It is easy to get swept up in a founder's passion for Italy without rigorously evaluating the business case underneath it.

**Why it's costly:** Personal passion without business infrastructure leads to frustrating, expensive engagements. The company establishes a legal entity, spends money on setup, and then cannot generate meaningful revenue because they never built a real go-to-market strategy or validated demand.

**The fix:** Separate the emotional motivation from the business readiness assessment. Acknowledge the personal passion, but evaluate the five readiness dimensions (`02_Expansion_Readiness.md`) objectively.

---

## Mistake 7: Proposing Before Discovering

**What it looks like:** After a brief initial conversation, the consultant rushes to put together a detailed proposal — complete with legal structure recommendations, pricing, and timelines — before fully understanding the prospect's situation.

**Why it happens:** Eagerness to "close" the opportunity. A belief that presenting a proposal quickly signals professionalism and responsiveness.

**Why it's costly:** A proposal sent before proper discovery is almost always wrong in at least one important way — scope too large, scope too small, wrong entity type, wrong service mix. A proposal that doesn't accurately reflect the prospect's situation is harder to defend and easier to reject.

**The fix:** Never send a substantive proposal until you have fully completed the discovery process and can answer all five dimensions of the scoring system with confidence.

---

## Mistake 8: Overpromising on Timelines or Outcomes

**What it looks like:** To close the deal or manage a prospect's impatience, the consultant implies that the company formation process is quick and simple ("We can have you up and running in a few weeks"), or promises outcomes that cannot be guaranteed ("You'll definitely be generating Italian revenue within six months").

**Why it happens:** Pressure to close, fear of saying something the prospect doesn't want to hear, or a lack of candor about the complexity of Italian bureaucracy.

**Why it's costly:** When timelines slip (as they frequently do in Italy) or outcomes fall short, the client blames ABAG rather than the inherent complexity of the market. Overpromising destroys trust and generates disputes.

**The fix:** Be honest and specific about timelines from the very first conversation. Set conservative expectations and then overdeliver. A client who knows upfront that company formation takes 8–12 weeks is far happier when it takes 10 weeks than a client who was told 4 weeks.

---

## Mistake 9: Neglecting the Follow-Up

**What it looks like:** An excellent discovery call is completed, but no follow-up email is sent that day. Days pass. The prospect moves on or forgets about the conversation. When the consultant finally reaches out, the energy from the call is gone.

**Why it happens:** Post-call fatigue, a busy schedule, or a belief that the prospect will follow up if they're interested.

**Why it's costly:** Most B2B buying decisions require multiple touchpoints. The follow-up after a first meeting is critical for maintaining momentum and demonstrating professionalism. A slow follow-up signals low priority or poor organization.

**The fix:** A personalized follow-up email should be sent within 4 hours of every discovery call. It should reference specific points from the conversation, confirm the agreed next step, and provide one piece of value (a relevant article, a short case study, or a simple framework).

---

## Mistake 10: Treating All Prospects the Same

**What it looks like:** The consultant uses the same script, the same questions, the same pitch, and the same proposal format for every prospect — regardless of industry, size, stage, or motivation.

**Why it happens:** Efficiency, habit, or a lack of preparation time.

**Why it's costly:** Generic outreach and generic conversations produce generic results. The best clients — the ones who become long-term strategic relationships — expect and reward genuine preparation and tailored engagement.

**The fix:** Spend at least 30 minutes preparing for every discovery call. Read the Company Intelligence Summary. Customize your opening statement. Select questions that are specific to that company's situation. Never use language like "as I do with all my clients" — every client should feel like ABAG's only client.

## Summary Table

| Mistake | Core Problem | Quick Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Talking too much | Learning nothing about the prospect | 40% talk rule |
| Skipping the budget question | Wasting time on non-buyers | Ask directly, frame professionally |
| Ignoring decision-maker mapping | Selling to someone with no authority | Identify stakeholders early |
| Misreading politeness as intent | False confidence in closing | Test commitment with specific questions |
| Dismissing early-stage prospects | Burning future relationships | Professional nurture, not dismissal |
| Confusing enthusiasm with readiness | Difficult, frustrating engagements | Assess readiness objectively |
| Proposing before discovering | Wrong-fit proposals | Complete discovery before proposing |
| Overpromising | Destroyed trust | Set conservative expectations |
| Slow follow-up | Lost momentum | Follow up within 4 hours |
| Generic approach | Mediocre results | Personalize every conversation |
