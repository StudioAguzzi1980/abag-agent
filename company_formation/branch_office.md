# Guide to Establishing an Italian Branch Office (Sede Secondaria)

## What Is an Italian Branch Office?

An Italian **branch office (sede secondaria)** is not a separate legal entity — it is a permanent extension of a foreign company operating in Italy. The foreign parent company retains **full legal and financial responsibility** for the branch's obligations.

A branch office is registered in Italy's Business Register and can carry out commercial activities, sign contracts, and employ staff. However, because it is not a separate entity, there is no separation of liability between the branch and the parent.

---

## Key Differences: Branch vs. Subsidiary

| Feature | Branch Office | Italian Subsidiary (S.r.l.) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal entity | No (extension of parent) | Yes (separate legal entity) |
| Liability | Parent bears full liability | Limited to the subsidiary's assets |
| Share capital | Not required | €10,000 minimum (standard S.r.l.) |
| Taxation | Italian-source profits taxed in Italy | Italian taxable entity; profits taxed in Italy |
| Director | Appointed representative (preposto) | Director appointed by shareholders |
| Complexity | Moderate | Moderate–high (more governance requirements) |
| Closing | Simpler to close | More formal liquidation process |

---

## When Is a Branch Office Appropriate?

A branch office may be appropriate when:
- The U.S. parent is willing to accept full liability for Italian operations
- The business needs to establish an Italian presence without the overhead of a separate entity
- Operations are expected to be relatively limited or time-bound
- The parent company's industry or structure makes a subsidiary unnecessarily complex

**Note:** Most ABAG clients ultimately choose an S.r.l. subsidiary because of the liability protection it offers. However, branch structures are sometimes used by larger U.S. corporations with strong balance sheets and specific operational reasons.

## Registration Process

### Step 1 — Appoint an Italian Representative (Preposto)

The branch must appoint a **preposto** — a representative residing in Italy with authority to represent the foreign company. The preposto does not need to be a shareholder, but must:
- Be a natural person (not a legal entity)
- Have an Italian tax code (codice fiscale)
- Accept the appointment in writing

### Step 2 — Prepare U.S. Parent Company Documents

The following documents are required from the U.S. parent:
- Certificate of Incorporation (apostilled and certified Italian translation)
- Articles of Incorporation/Bylaws (apostilled and certified Italian translation)
- Certificate of Good Standing or Incumbency Certificate (apostilled and certified Italian translation)
- Board resolution authorizing the establishment of the Italian branch and appointing the preposto
- Power of attorney for the Italian notary or representative, if required

**Apostille:** All U.S. company documents must carry an apostille issued by the Secretary of State of the incorporating state.

### Step 3 — Italian Notarial Deed

The registration of the branch office requires a deed before an Italian notary. The notary will review the foreign company documents and complete the registration with the Chamber of Commerce.

### Step 4 — Chamber of Commerce Registration

The branch is registered in the Registro delle Imprese. The branch will receive its own Italian fiscal code and may be assigned a VAT number.

### Step 5 — VAT Registration

If the branch will make taxable supplies in Italy, it must register for VAT (Partita IVA) with the Agenzia delle Entrate.

### Step 6 — INPS/INAIL Registration

If employees will be hired through the branch, register with INPS and INAIL.

## Taxation of an Italian Branch

An Italian branch is subject to Italian tax on its **Italian-source income** (profits attributable to the branch's activities):

- **IRES:** 24% on the branch's taxable profits
- **IRAP:** 3.9% on the production value
- The branch must file a separate Italian tax return reporting profits attributable to Italian operations

The U.S. parent must also report the branch income in its U.S. tax return. The U.S.–Italy Tax Treaty provides for a foreign tax credit to avoid double taxation.

**Transfer pricing** applies to transactions between the branch and the parent, following the OECD functionally separate entity approach.

---

## Compliance Obligations

Italian branches have ongoing compliance obligations similar to Italian companies:

- Filing of annual financial statements (if the branch is of sufficient size)
- VAT return filings
- Payroll tax and INPS contributions
- Update of Business Register for any changes
- Maintenance of the preposto appointment

---

## Closing an Italian Branch

Closing a branch office is generally simpler than liquidating an Italian subsidiary:
1. Board resolution of U.S. parent authorizing closure
2. Notarial deed filed with the Chamber of Commerce
3. Cancellation of VAT number and other registrations
4. Settlement of all outstanding liabilities (tax, employees, suppliers)
