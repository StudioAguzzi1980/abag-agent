# GDPR Compliance Guide for Italian Entities

## Overview

The **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR — EU Regulation 2016/679)** is the primary data protection law applicable in Italy and across the European Union. It entered into force on May 25, 2018, and applies to any organization that processes the personal data of individuals in the EU — regardless of where the organization is established.

For U.S.-owned Italian companies, GDPR compliance is mandatory and carries significant penalties for non-compliance. Italy's supervisory authority, the **Garante per la Protezione dei Dati Personali** (Garante Privacy), actively enforces GDPR requirements.

---

## Key GDPR Principles

| Principle | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency** | Personal data must be processed on a valid legal basis; individuals must be informed |
| **Purpose limitation** | Data collected for a specific purpose cannot be used for other purposes |
| **Data minimization** | Only collect data that is necessary for the stated purpose |
| **Accuracy** | Personal data must be accurate and kept up to date |
| **Storage limitation** | Data should not be kept longer than necessary |
| **Integrity and confidentiality** | Appropriate security measures must protect personal data |
| **Accountability** | The data controller must be able to demonstrate compliance |

---

## Legal Bases for Processing

Processing personal data requires one of the following legal bases:

1. **Consent** — Freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous
2. **Contractual necessity** — Processing necessary for performing a contract with the individual
3. **Legal obligation** — Processing required to comply with a legal obligation
4. **Vital interests** — Protecting life
5. **Public task** — Relevant for public authorities
6. **Legitimate interests** — The organization's legitimate interests, provided not overridden by the individual's rights

For most business activities, the applicable bases are contractual necessity, legal obligation, and legitimate interests.

## Key GDPR Obligations for Italian Companies

### 1. Privacy Policy (Informativa Privacy)

Every Italian company must provide individuals with a clear privacy notice (informativa) when collecting their data. This applies to:
- Employees and job applicants
- Clients and suppliers
- Website visitors
- Contacts acquired through marketing

**Required information:**
- Identity and contact details of the data controller
- Purpose and legal basis of processing
- Categories of data processed
- Recipients of the data (including third parties)
- Retention periods
- Individual rights and how to exercise them
- Right to complain to the Garante

### 2. Records of Processing Activities (RoPA)

Companies with ≥250 employees (and in some cases smaller companies) must maintain **records of all processing activities** (Article 30).

Even if not strictly required by size, maintaining a RoPA is best practice and evidence of accountability.

The RoPA must include:
- Name and contact details of the controller
- Purposes of processing
- Categories of data subjects and personal data
- Recipients of personal data
- Transfers to third countries
- Retention periods
- Security measures

### 3. Data Processing Agreements (DPAs)

When sharing personal data with third-party processors (e.g., payroll providers, accounting software, marketing platforms), a formal **Data Processing Agreement** (DPA) is required under Article 28.

Common processors requiring a DPA:
- Payroll processing services
- Accounting / ERP software providers
- Email marketing platforms
- Cloud service providers
- HR management systems

### 4. Data Breach Response

In the event of a personal data breach:
- **Notify the Garante Privacy within 72 hours** (if the breach poses a risk to individuals' rights)
- Notify affected individuals if the breach is high-risk
- Document the breach and response in the breach register

### 5. Data Subject Rights

Individuals have the following rights under GDPR, which the company must be prepared to honor:

| Right | Description |
|---|---|
| **Access** | Right to obtain a copy of their personal data |
| **Rectification** | Right to correct inaccurate data |
| **Erasure ("Right to be forgotten")** | Right to delete data in certain circumstances |
| **Restriction** | Right to limit processing in certain circumstances |
| **Portability** | Right to receive data in a machine-readable format |
| **Object** | Right to object to processing based on legitimate interests |
| **Automated decisions** | Right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with significant effect |

Response deadline: **within 1 month** of receiving the request.

### 6. Data Protection Officer (DPO)

A DPO is **mandatory** for:
- Public authorities
- Organizations conducting large-scale systematic monitoring of individuals
- Organizations processing special categories of data (health, racial origin, etc.) on a large scale

For most standard S.r.l. businesses, a DPO is not mandatory — but appointing a DPO voluntarily demonstrates accountability and simplifies compliance.

## Italian-Specific Considerations

Italy's Garante Privacy has been one of the most active GDPR supervisory authorities in the EU, with notable enforcement actions against:
- Google (international data transfers)
- OpenAI (ChatGPT — temporary ban)
- WhatsApp
- Various Italian businesses for employee monitoring and cookie practices

Italian companies should pay particular attention to:
- **Cookie consent** — Italy has strict rules on cookie banners and consent mechanisms
- **Employee monitoring** — Strict limits on monitoring employees in Italy (Art. 4 of the Workers' Statute applies)
- **International transfers** — Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) are required for data transfers to the U.S.

## GDPR Penalties

- **Minor infringements:** Up to €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover
- **Major infringements:** Up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover
