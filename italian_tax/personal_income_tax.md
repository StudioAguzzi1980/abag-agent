# Italian Personal Income Tax and Expatriate Taxation

## Overview

For American executives, employees, or entrepreneurs relocating to Italy — or spending significant time working there — understanding Italian personal income tax (IRPEF) is essential. Italy's personal tax system interacts with U.S. tax obligations in complex ways that require coordinated planning.

---

## IRPEF — Imposta sul Reddito delle Persone Fisiche

IRPEF is Italy's progressive personal income tax, levied on the worldwide income of **Italian tax residents** and on Italian-source income of **non-residents**.

### IRPEF Tax Brackets (2024 reference)

| Taxable Income | Tax Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to €28,000 | 23% |
| €28,001 – €50,000 | 35% |
| Over €50,000 | 43% |

*Note: Additional regional (addizionale regionale) and municipal (addizionale comunale) surcharges apply on top of IRPEF. These vary by region and municipality and typically add 1–3%.*

---

## Tax Residency in Italy

An individual is considered an Italian tax resident for any calendar year in which, for more than **183 days** in that year (184 in leap years), any one of the following applies:
- The individual is **registered** in the Italian Register of the Resident Population (Anagrafe)
- The individual has their **domicile** (centro degli interessi vitali) in Italy
- The individual has their **residence** (habitually resides) in Italy

Italian tax residents are taxed on their **worldwide income**. This creates potential double taxation for U.S. citizens/residents who also pay U.S. tax on worldwide income — addressed by the U.S.–Italy Tax Treaty and foreign tax credits.

## Italian Flat Tax for New Residents (Regime dei Neo-Residenti)

Italy offers a significant incentive for high-net-worth individuals transferring their tax residency to Italy:

### Who Qualifies
- Individuals who have not been Italian tax residents for at least 9 of the 10 tax years preceding their application
- No income level threshold to qualify (but designed for high-net-worth individuals)
- Includes non-Italian nationals; particularly relevant for U.S. citizens relocating to Italy

### How It Works
- **€100,000 per year flat tax** on all **foreign-source income** (regardless of amount)
- Italian-source income is taxed normally under IRPEF
- The flat tax replaces IRPEF on foreign-source income — a massive benefit for those with significant U.S. investment income, dividends, rental income, capital gains, etc.
- **Additional family members** can opt in for **€25,000 per person per year** flat tax
- Duration: **Up to 15 years**
- Opting in: Application submitted to the Agenzia delle Entrate; advance tax ruling (interpello) available to confirm eligibility

### What Is "Foreign-Source Income"?
- U.S. dividends and capital gains
- U.S. rental income
- U.S. business income from a U.S. entity
- Foreign investment income generally
- Note: Income from an Italian business or employer is Italian-source income and taxed normally

## Impatriate Workers Regime (Regime degli Impatriati)

For employees or self-employed individuals relocating to Italy to work:

### Key Benefits (from 2024 rules)
- **50% income exemption** for employment or self-employment income for the first 5 years of Italian tax residency
- Applicable to individuals who have not been resident in Italy for the prior 3 tax years (or 6 years for workers from certain countries)
- Applies only to income from Italian employment or self-employment
- Income cap and other conditions apply — confirm current rules with ABAG

This regime is particularly relevant for:
- American professionals accepting employment in Italy
- U.S. company employees relocated to the Italian subsidiary
- Founders of Italian startups relocating to Italy

## U.S. Tax Obligations for Americans in Italy

U.S. citizens and permanent residents (green card holders) remain subject to U.S. tax on **worldwide income** regardless of where they live. This creates a dual-tax obligation for Americans in Italy.

### Key U.S. Filing Obligations

| Form | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Form 1040** | Annual U.S. individual income tax return |
| **FBAR (FinCEN 114)** | Disclosure of foreign bank accounts exceeding $10,000 |
| **Form 8938 (FATCA)** | Disclosure of foreign financial assets above thresholds |
| **Form 2555** | Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (up to ~$120,000/year excluded from U.S. income tax) |
| **Form 1116** | Foreign Tax Credit — credit for Italian taxes against U.S. tax liability |
| **Form 3520** | Disclosure of transactions with foreign trusts or large foreign gifts |

### Avoiding Double Taxation

The primary mechanisms for U.S. citizens in Italy to avoid double taxation are:
1. **Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116)** — Credit Italian IRPEF paid against U.S. income tax on the same income
2. **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555)** — Exclude a portion of earned (employment/self-employment) income from U.S. tax
3. **Treaty provisions** — Article 24 of the U.S.–Italy Treaty governs elimination of double taxation

ABAG recommends that U.S. citizens relocating to Italy coordinate with a U.S. CPA experienced in expatriate taxation to ensure full compliance on both sides of the Atlantic.
